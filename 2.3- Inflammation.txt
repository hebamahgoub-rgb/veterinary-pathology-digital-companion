      <h2>1. Start Here: Why and How Tissues Respond to Injury</h2>
      <p>Inflammation is a fundamental protective response of vascularized living tissue to injury. Its objective is to dilute, destroy, or isolate the offending agent, clear necrotic debris, and initiate tissue repair. Without inflammation, infections would spread uncontrollably and wounds would never heal. However, when excessive, persistent, or misdirected, inflammation becomes a primary cause of tissue destruction and clinical disease.</p>
    </div>

    <!-- 2. IMMUNITY BACKGROUND -->
    <section id="immunity" class="topic-section">
      <h2>2. Immunity Background: Before You Study Inflammation</h2>
      <p>Inflammation is closely linked to the immune system. A clear understanding of innate and adaptive immunity helps explain how tissues recognize injury, recruit inflammatory cells, produce chemical mediators, eliminate harmful agents, and eventually resolve or repair damage.</p>

      <div class="pattern-grid">
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Innate Immunity</h3>
          <p>The rapid, non-specific first line of defense. It includes epithelial barriers, phagocytic cells, natural killer cells, complement proteins, pattern-recognition receptors, and inflammatory mediators.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Adaptive Immunity</h3>
          <p>A slower but highly specific response mediated mainly by lymphocytes. B cells generate antibody-mediated immunity, while T cells coordinate cellular immune responses and regulate inflammation.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Recognition and Signaling</h3>
          <p>Immune and tissue cells detect microbial structures and molecules released from damaged cells. This recognition triggers cytokine production, leukocyte recruitment, and activation of vascular and cellular inflammatory responses.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Why It Matters in Pathology</h3>
          <p>Protective immune responses remove harmful agents, but excessive, persistent, or misdirected responses can produce tissue injury, chronic inflammation, hypersensitivity, and immune-mediated disease.</p>
        </div>
      </div>

    <!-- 3. DEFINITION, CAUSES AND CARDINAL SIGNS -->
    <section id="definition" class="topic-section">
      <h2>3. Definition, Causes and Cardinal Signs</h2>
      <p><strong>Definition:</strong> A complex immunovascular reaction of microcirculation and parenchymal tissue to local injury.</p>
      
      <h3>Causes of Inflammation</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Infectious Agents:</strong> Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites, and microbial toxins.</li>
        <li><strong>Physical Factors:</strong> Trauma, extreme heat or cold, ionizing radiation, mechanical pressure.</li>
        <li><strong>Chemical Factors:</strong> Corrosive acids/alkalis, endogenous metabolic toxins (e.g., uremic toxins), irritants.</li>
        <li><strong>Tissue Necrosis:</strong> Ischemic necrosis (infarction), trauma, chemical injury.</li>
        <li><strong>Immunologic Reactions:</strong> Autoimmune diseases, hypersensitivity (allergic) reactions, immune complex deposition.</li>
      </ul>

    <!-- 5. VASCULAR EVENTS -->
    <section id="vascular" class="topic-section">
      <h2>5. Vascular Events</h2>
      <p>Microvascular alterations occur in a distinct, orchestrated sequence:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Transient Arteriolar Vasoconstriction:</strong> Neurogenic reflex lasting a few seconds to minutes.</li>
        <li><strong>Arteriolar Vasodilation:</strong> Mediated by histamine and nitric oxide, causing active hyperemia (redness and heat).</li>
        <li><strong>Increased Microvascular Permeability:</strong> Endothelial cell contraction (mediated by histamine, leukotrienes) creates interendothelial gaps, leading to protein-rich fluid exudation into tissues.</li>
        <li><strong>Stasis &amp; Leukocyte Margination:</strong> Loss of fluid increases blood viscosity and slows circulation, allowing leukocytes to settle along the endothelial margin.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>Exudate vs. Transudate</h3>
      <table class="data-table">
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Feature</th>
            <th>Exudate</th>
            <th>Transudate</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Definition</strong></td>
            <td>Inflammatory extravascular fluid</td>
            <td>Non-inflammatory extravascular fluid</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Protein Content</strong></td>
            <td>High (&gt; 30 g/L)</td>
            <td>Low (&lt; 30 g/L)</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Specific Gravity</strong></td>
            <td>High (&gt; 1.020)</td>
            <td>Low (&lt; 1.012)</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Cellular Content</strong></td>
            <td>High (abundant leukocytes/neutrophils)</td>
            <td>Low (few mesothelial or mononuclear cells)</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Endothelial Permeability</strong></td>
            <td>Increased (increased permeability)</td>
            <td>Normal (hydrostatic / oncotic shift)</td>
          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </section>

    <!-- 6. LEUKOCYTE RECRUITMENT -->
    <section id="leukocyte" class="topic-section">
      <h2>6. Leukocyte Recruitment</h2>
      <p>The movement of leukocytes from the vessel lumen to the tissue site of injury proceeds via a multi-step cascade:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Margination and Rolling:</strong> Weak, transient leukocyte-endothelial interactions mediated by <em>Selectins</em> (E-selectin, P-selectin, L-selectin).</li>
        <li><strong>Firm Adhesion:</strong> High-affinity binding mediated by leukocyte <em>Integrins</em> (LFA-1, Mac-1) binding to endothelial adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1).</li>
        <li><strong>Transmigration (Diapedesis):</strong> Movement between endothelial cells mediated by <em>PECAM-1 (CD31)</em>, followed by basement membrane degradation via collagenases.</li>
        <li><strong>Chemotaxis:</strong> Directed migration along a chemical concentration gradient toward the injury site (mediated by C5a, LTB4, IL-8, bacterial products).</li>
        <li><strong>Phagocytosis &amp; Killing:</strong> Recognition/attachment (opsonization by IgG, C3b), engulfment (phagosome formation), and intracellular killing (ROS, myeloperoxidase system).</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 7. INFLAMMATORY MEDIATORS -->
    <section id="mediators" class="topic-section">
      <h2>7. Inflammatory Mediators</h2>
      <p>Chemical substances originating from plasma proteins or cellular sources that initiate and regulate inflammatory responses.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Vasoactive Amines (Histamine, Serotonin):</strong> Derived from mast cells, basophils, platelets; cause rapid vasodilation and vascular permeability.</li>
        <li><strong>Arachidonic Acid Metabolites (Eicosanoids):</strong>
          <ul>
            <li><em>Prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2):</em> Cause vasodilation, fever, and pain.</li>
            <li><em>Leukotrienes (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4):</em> LTB4 causes chemotaxis; LTC4/D4 cause intense vasoconstriction, bronchospasm, and increased permeability.</li>
          </ul>
        </li>
        <li><strong>Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6):</strong> Derived from macrophages; mediate local endothelial activation and systemic acute-phase reactions (fever, lethargy).</li>
        <li><strong>Complement System (C3a, C5a):</strong> C3a and C5a act as anaphylatoxins (triggering histamine release); C5a is a potent neutrophil chemoattractant. C5b-9 forms the membrane attack complex (MAC).</li>
        <li><strong>Kinin System (Bradykinin):</strong> Increases vascular permeability, smooth muscle contraction, and induces pain.</li>
      </ul>

    <!-- 8. MORPHOLOGICAL PATTERNS -->
    <section id="patterns" class="topic-section">
      <h2>8. Morphological Patterns of Inflammation</h2>
      <p>The predominant nature of the exudate and tissue response determines the specific morphological classification:</p>
      
      <div class="pattern-grid">
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Serous Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Characterized by the outpouring of a thin, watery fluid (serous exudate or transudate-like) low in protein and cells. Derived from plasma or mesothelial secretions. <em>Examples: Skin blisters, serous pericarditis/pleuritis.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Catarrhal Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Occurs on mucous membranes rich in goblet cells (respiratory, gastrointestinal tracts). Characterized by hypersecretion of thick, clear, or cloudy mucus mixed with WBCs and desquamated epithelial cells. <em>Example: Acute rhinitis.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Fibrinous Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Occurs when vascular leakage is severe, allowing large fibrinogen molecules to pass into tissues. Fibrinogen converts to fibrin, forming a dull, rough, shaggy, or elastic mat. <em>Example: Fibrinous pericarditis ("bread-and-butter"), fibrinous pleuropneumonia.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Suppurative (Purulent) Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Characterized by the production of <strong>pus</strong> (purulent exudate consisting of viable and necrotic neutrophils, cellular debris, and edema fluid). Frequently caused by pyogenic bacteria (e.g., <em>Staphylococcus</em>, <em>Streptococcus</em>). <em>Examples: Abscesses, phlegmon, empyema.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Hemorrhagic Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Severe vascular injury resulting in widespread rupture or extreme permeability of blood vessels, leading to massive extravasation of RBCs into tissues alongside inflammatory exudate. <em>Examples: Anthrax, Blackleg (Clostridium chauvoei), Canine parvovirus enteritis.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Necrotizing Inflammation</h3>
          <p>Severe tissue death (necrosis) accompanies the inflammatory exudate due to high-virulence toxins or severe vascular compromise. <em>Examples: Necrotizing enteritis, necrotizing fasciitis.</em></p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Granulomatous Inflammation</h3>
          <p>A distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation characterized by focal collections of modified macrophages (<strong>epithelioid macrophages</strong>) surrounded by a rim of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and multinucleated giant cells. <em>Examples: Tuberculosis, Johne's disease.</em></p>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- 9. OUTCOMES OF ACUTE INFLAMMATION -->
    <section id="outcomes" class="topic-section">
      <h2>9. Outcomes of Acute Inflammation</h2>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Complete Resolution:</strong> Perfect restoration of normal tissue structure and function (occurs when tissue destruction is minimal, offending agent is eliminated, and tissue has high regenerative capacity).</li>
        <li><strong>Healing by Fibrosis / Scarring:</strong> Occurs following substantial tissue destruction, in non-regenerative tissues, or when abundant fibrinous exudate cannot be cleared (organization by granulation tissue).</li>
        <li><strong>Progression to Chronic Inflammation:</strong> Occurs when the offending agent persists, or due to interference with the normal healing sequence.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 10. CHRONIC INFLAMMATION -->
    <section id="chronic" class="topic-section">
      <h2>10. Chronic Inflammation</h2>
      <p>Inflammation of prolonged duration (weeks, months, or years) in which active tissue destruction, inflammatory cell infiltration, and attempts at healing (fibrosis) proceed simultaneously.</p>
      
      <h3>Key Hallmarks of Chronic Inflammation</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Mononuclear Infiltration:</strong> Predominance of <strong>macrophages</strong>, <strong>lymphocytes</strong>, and <strong>plasma cells</strong> (instead of neutrophils).</li>
        <li><strong>Tissue Destruction (Tissue Injury):</strong> Induced largely by persistent inflammatory cells.</li>
        <li><strong>Attempts at Healing:</strong> Proliferation of small blood vessels (angiogenesis) and deposition of connective tissue (fibrosis/fibroplasia).</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 11. GRANULOMATOUS INFLAMMATION -->
    <section id="granulomatous" class="topic-section">
      <h2>11. Granulomatous Inflammation</h2>
      <p>A specialized form of chronic inflammation designed to isolate indigestible or persistent infectious or non-infectious agents.</p>
      
      <h3>Components of a Granuloma</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Epithelioid Macrophages:</strong> Macrophages with abundant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm resembling epithelial cells; specialized for secretion rather than phagocytosis.</li>
        <li><strong>Multinucleated Giant Cells:</strong> Fusion of epithelioid cells forming <em>Langhans giant cells</em> (horseshoe nuclei peripheral arrangement; typical of TB) or <em>Foreign-body giant cells</em> (haphazard nuclei arrangement).</li>
        <li><strong>Lymphocytic &amp; Plasma Cell Rim:</strong> Encircling antigen-presenting macrophages.</li>
        <li><strong>Fibrous Capsule:</strong> Outer connective tissue wall isolating the core.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 12. SYSTEMIC EFFECTS -->
    <section id="systemic" class="topic-section">
      <h2>12. Systemic Effects of Inflammation</h2>
      <p>Cytokine-mediated responses (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) operating systemically are termed the <strong>Acute-Phase Reaction</strong>:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Fever:</strong> Induced by pyrogens stimulating prostaglandin synthesis (PGE2) in the hypothalamus.</li>
        <li><strong>Leukocytosis:</strong> Increased circulating leukocyte count (neutrophilia in bacterial infection, lymphocytosis in viral, eosinophilia in parasitic/allergic).</li>
        <li><strong>Acute-Phase Protein Synthesis:</strong> Markedly elevated hepatic synthesis of proteins like C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, and serum amyloid A (SAA).</li>
        <li><strong>Systemic Signs:</strong> Lethargy, anorexia, somnolence, tachycardia, hypotension (in septic shock).</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 13. HEALING AND REPAIR -->
    <section id="repair" class="topic-section">
      <h2>13. Healing and Repair</h2>
      <p>Restoration of tissue architecture and function following injury occurs via two mechanisms:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Regeneration:</strong> Proliferation of residual uninjured parenchymal cells to restore original architecture (requires intact extracellular matrix and labile/stable cell types).</li>
        <li><strong>Repair by Fibrosis (Scar Formation):</strong> Replacement of injured parenchymal cells by connective tissue.</li>
      </ul>

      <h3>Steps in Scar Formation</h3>
      <ol style="margin-left: 20px; margin-bottom: 15px;">
        <li><strong>Hemostatic Plug &amp; Inflammation:</strong> Clotting and neutrophil/macrophage recruitment to clear debris.</li>
        <li><strong>Granulation Tissue Formation:</strong> Proliferation of thin-walled delicate capillaries (angiogenesis) + proliferating fibroblasts in a loose ECM. Grossly soft, pink, granular.</li>
        <li><strong>Tissue Remodeling:</strong> Collagen synthesis (Type I replacing Type III) and maturation, vascular regression, and scar contraction (mediated by myofibroblasts).</li>
      </ol>
