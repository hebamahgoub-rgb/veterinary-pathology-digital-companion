      <h2>1. Start Here: Maintaining Vascular Flow and Tissue Perfusion</h2>
      <p>Health requires a continuous supply of oxygenated blood delivered under appropriate pressure, fluid volume homeostasis across microvascular beds, and an intact intravascular coagulation equilibrium. Disturbances in fluid balance or vascular patency result in cell hypoxia, metabolic dysfunction, tissue swelling, or systemic collapse.</p>

    <!-- 2. HYPEREMIA AND CONGESTION -->
    <section id="hyperemia" class="topic-section">
      <h2>2. Hyperemia and Congestion</h2>
      <p>Both terms indicate an increased volume of blood within a particular tissue or organ, but stem from fundamentally different pathophysiological mechanisms:</p>

      <table class="data-table">
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Feature</th>
            <th>Hyperemia</th>
            <th>Congestion</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Process Type</strong></td>
            <td><strong>Active</strong> process</td>
            <td><strong>Passive</strong> process</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Mechanism</strong></td>
            <td>Arteriolar vasodilation increasing arterial inflow.</td>
            <td>Impaired venous outflow from tissue.</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Common Causes</strong></td>
            <td>Inflammation, exercise, neurogenic regulation.</td>
            <td>Heart failure, venous obstruction (thrombus/mass), torsion.</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Gross Appearance</strong></td>
            <td>Bright red, warm, engorged tissue.</td>
            <td>Dark red to cyanotic (blue-purple), cool, swollen tissue.</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Microscopic Features</strong></td>
            <td>Capillaries engorged with oxygenated RBCs.</td>
            <td>Dilated veins/capillaries; chronic cases show hemosiderophages.</td>
          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1hsYFrr9PQXsRfBzrlgAc7yWXB48XXETy" alt="Illustration comparing active hyperemia with passive congestion" loading="lazy">
        <figcaption><strong>Hyperemia vs. Congestion.</strong> Active hyperemia results from increased arterial inflow, producing bright red tissue. Congestion results from impaired venous outflow, producing dark red-blue tissue. These changes reflect different hemodynamic mechanisms but both represent abnormal blood volume within tissues.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </section>

    <!-- 3. EDEMA -->
    <section id="edema" class="topic-section">
      <h2>3. Edema</h2>
      <p>An abnormal accumulation of fluid within interstitial tissue spaces or body cavities (e.g., hydrothorax, hydropericardium, ascites). Governed by Starling forces across microvascular membranes.</p>

      <h3>Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Edema</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Increased Intravascular Hydrostatic Pressure:</strong> Impaired venous return (e.g., congestive heart failure, venous thrombosis, portal hypertension).</li>
        <li><strong>Decreased Plasma Oncotic Pressure (Hypoproteinemia):</strong> Loss of plasma albumin (e.g., protein-losing nephropathy/enteropathy) or decreased liver synthesis (hepatic failure, starvation).</li>
        <li><strong>Increased Vascular Permeability:</strong> Endothelial gap formation induced by inflammatory mediators (e.g., histamine, bradykinin, cytokines).</li>
        <li><strong>Lymphatic Obstruction (Lymphedema):</strong> Impaired lymphatic drainage due to neoplastic compression, surgical disruption, or trauma.</li>
        <li><strong>Sodium and Water Retention:</strong> Secondary to reduced renal perfusion activating the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS).</li>
      </ul>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1CGpYjfQvhe80E_RTqxTbUScw71AJxluv" alt="Illustration of the major mechanisms of edema formation" loading="lazy">
        <figcaption><strong>Mechanisms of Edema.</strong> Edema develops when fluid accumulates in the interstitium due to increased hydrostatic pressure, reduced oncotic pressure, increased vascular permeability, or lymphatic obstruction. These mechanisms disrupt normal Starling equilibrium and promote fluid leakage from the microvasculature.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </section>

    <!-- 4. HEMORRHAGE -->
    <section id="hemorrhage" class="topic-section">
      <h2>4. Hemorrhage</h2>
      <p>Extravasation of blood from the vascular system into tissues, body cavities, or externally, resulting from vessel rupture (rhexis) or leakage through intact endothelium (diapedesis).</p>

      <h3>Morphological Classification by Size</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Petechiae:</strong> Minute pin-point hemorrhages (1–2 mm) typically associated with thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, or endothelial damage.</li>
        <li><strong>Purpura:</strong> Slightly larger hemorrhagic foci (3–5 mm).</li>
        <li><strong>Ecchymoses:</strong> Larger blotchy hemorrhages (&gt; 1–2 cm); classic "bruises."</li>
        <li><strong>Suffusion:</strong> Flat, contiguous, widespread area of tissue hemorrhage.</li>
        <li><strong>Hematoma:</strong> A focal 3D accumulation of blood that forms a palpable mass within a tissue or cavity.</li>
      </ul>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1hZ-TYF4roOPby45kGRDgTiVRMMjHMR_R" alt="Illustration of morphological types of hemorrhage" loading="lazy">
        <figcaption><strong>Morphological Types of Hemorrhage.</strong> Hemorrhage may appear as petechiae, ecchymoses, suffusions, or hematomas, depending on the extent and distribution of blood loss. Recognizing these patterns aids in identifying underlying causes such as trauma, coagulopathies, or vascular injury.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </section>

    <!-- 5. HEMOSTASIS -->
    <section id="hemostasis" class="topic-section">
      <h2>5. Hemostasis</h2>
      <p>A tightly regulated physiological process that maintains blood in a fluid state within normal vessels, while rapidly inducing a localized hemostatic plug at sites of vascular injury.</p>

      <ol style="margin-left: 20px; margin-bottom: 15px;">
        <li><strong>Arteriolar Vasoconstriction:</strong> Immediate local reflex and endothelin release.</li>
        <li><strong>Primary Hemostasis:</strong> Endothelial exposure of subendothelial collagen &rarr; von Willebrand factor (vWF) binding &rarr; platelet adhesion, activation, shape change, and aggregation forming a primary platelet plug.</li>
        <li><strong>Secondary Hemostasis:</strong> Tissue factor exposure activates the coagulation cascade, culminating in thrombin generation which cleaves fibrinogen into an insoluble fibrin mesh.</li>
        <li><strong>Fibrinolysis &amp; Anticoagulation:</strong> Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) activates plasmin to limit clot growth and clear the thrombus during tissue repair.</li>
      </ol>
    </section>

    <!-- 6. THROMBOSIS -->
    <section id="thrombosis" class="topic-section">
      <h2>6. Thrombosis</h2>
      <p>The inappropriate or excessive intravascular formation of a solid mass (thrombus) composed of fibrin, platelets, and blood cells inside a living blood vessel.</p>

      <h3>Virchow's Triad</h3>
      <div class="feature-grid">
        <div class="feature-card">
          <h3>1. Endothelial Injury</h3>
          <p>The dominant factor. Exposure of subendothelial collagen and tissue factor (e.g., vasculitis, arteriosclerosis, catheter trauma).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="feature-card">
          <h3>2. Alterations in Blood Flow</h3>
          <p>Stasis or turbulence disrupting laminar flow, preventing dilution of clotting factors and bringing platelets into contact with endothelium.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="feature-card">
          <h3>3. Hypercoagulability</h3>
          <p>Primary or secondary alterations in the clotting system (e.g., Antithrombin III loss in protein-losing nephropathy, neoplasia, sepsis).</p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <h3>Thrombus vs. Postmortem Clot</h3>
      <table class="data-table">
        <thead>
          <tr>
            <th>Feature</th>
            <th>Arterial / Venous Thrombus</th>
            <th>Postmortem Clot</th>
          </tr>
        </thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Attachment</strong></td>
            <td>Firmly attached to vessel wall</td>
            <td>Unattached; easily removed</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Texture &amp; Appearance</strong></td>
            <td>Dull, dry, friable; lines of Zahn</td>
            <td>Shiny, smooth, elastic, gelatinous</td>
          </tr>
          <tr>
            <td><strong>Color Pattern</strong></td>
            <td>Laminated red/pale layers</td>
            <td>"Currant jelly" (red bottom) &amp; "Chicken fat" (yellow top)</td>
          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1Ta3eAX6E6GRMW1_DOL93orRB9w7ZGKNf" alt="Illustration of thrombus formation and possible outcomes" loading="lazy">
        <figcaption><strong>Formation and Fate of a Thrombus.</strong> Thrombosis begins with endothelial injury, platelet adhesion, and fibrin deposition. A thrombus may resolve, embolize, organize, or undergo recanalization. These outcomes determine the clinical significance of thrombosis in animals.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </section>

    <!-- 7. EMBOLISM -->
    <section id="embolism" class="topic-section">
      <h2>7. Embolism</h2>
      <p>An intravascular solid, liquid, or gaseous mass carried by the blood stream from its point of origin to a distant site, where it lodges and causes vascular obstruction.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Thromboembolism:</strong> Dislodged thrombus fragments (most common type).</li>
        <li><strong>Fat Embolism:</strong> Bone marrow/fat emboli following long-bone fractures or trauma.</li>
        <li><strong>Bacterial / Parasitic Embolism:</strong> Septic emboli (e.g., vegetative endocarditis, <em>Dirofilaria immitis</em>).</li>
        <li><strong>Gas / Air Embolism:</strong> Sudden pressure decompression or accidental intravenous air injection.</li>
        <li><strong>Fibrocartilaginous Embolism (FCE):</strong> Degenerated intervertebral disc material entering spinal cord vasculature.</li>
      </ul>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1WX8c0gWHDarpCJICaPNlxfRIw719avCj" alt="Illustration showing types of emboli and their consequences" loading="lazy">
        <figcaption><strong>Types of Emboli and Their Effects.</strong> Emboli may consist of thrombi, fat, air, bacteria, or tumor cells. When they obstruct downstream vessels, they can cause ischemia and infarction. The nature of the embolus influences both the clinical presentation and pathological outcome.</figcaption>
      </figure>
    </section>

    <!-- 8. ISCHEMIA -->
    <section id="ischemia" class="topic-section">
      <h2>8. Ischemia</h2>
      <p>Inadequate arterial blood supply to a tissue relative to its metabolic demands. Unlike hypoxia alone (lack of oxygen), ischemia deprives tissue of both oxygen and metabolic substrates (glucose) while allowing toxic metabolic wastes (lactic acid) to accumulate.</p>
    </section>

    <!-- 9. INFARCTION -->
    <section id="infarction" class="topic-section">
      <h2>9. Infarction</h2>
      <p>An area of ischemic coagulative or liquefactive necrosis caused by occlusion of either the arterial supply or the venous drainage of an organ or tissue.</p>

      <h3>Classification of Infarcts</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Red (Hemorrhagic) Infarcts:</strong> Occur in venous occlusions, loose spongy tissues (e.g., lung), tissues with dual blood supply (e.g., liver, intestine), or upon reperfusion of previously ischemic tissue.</li>
        <li><strong>White (Anemic / Pale) Infarcts:</strong> Occur with arterial occlusions in solid parenchymal organs with end-arterial circulations (e.g., kidney, heart, spleen), where tissue density limits hemorrhage inflow.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 10. SHOCK -->
    <section id="shock" class="topic-section">
      <h2>10. Shock</h2>
      <p>A state of systemic hypoperfusion resulting from decreased effective circulating blood volume or cardiac output, leading to cellular hypoxia, anaerobic metabolism, and multiorgan breakdown.</p>

      <h3>Categories of Shock</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Cardiogenic Shock:</strong> Failure of myocardial pump function (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy, severe arrhythmias, cardiac tamponade).</li>
        <li><strong>Hypovolemic Shock:</strong> Acute loss of blood or plasma volume (e.g., massive hemorrhage, severe dehydration/diarrhea).</li>
        <li><strong>Distributive Shock:</strong> Systemic vasodilation and severe loss of peripheral vascular resistance:
          <ul>
            <li><em>Septic Shock:</em> Endotoxin/microbial stimulation releasing cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1).</li>
            <li><em>Anaphylactic Shock:</em> IgE-mediated systemic mast cell degranulation (histamine release).</li>
            <li><em>Neurogenic Shock:</em> Loss of vascular tone following spinal trauma.</li>
          </ul>
        </li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 11. DIC -->
    <section id="dic" class="topic-section">
      <h2>11. Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)</h2>
      <p>A grave, acquired clinicopathological syndrome characterized by widespread systemic activation of the coagulation cascade across the microvasculature. Leads to microthrombi formation, organ ischemia, and rapid consumption of platelets and clotting factors, culminating in uncontrolled widespread bleeding (consumption coagulopathy).</p>
    </section>
