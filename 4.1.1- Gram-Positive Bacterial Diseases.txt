      <h1>Gram-Positive Bacterial Diseases</h1>
      <div class="hero-subtitle">Pyogenic cocci, coryneform bacilli, and spore-forming anaerobes</div>
      <p class="hero-body">
        Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick peptidoglycan cell wall that retains crystal violet stain. In veterinary pathology, these organisms frequently induce suppurative abscessation, necrotising enteritis, exotoxin-mediated systemic tissue injury, or granulomatous lesions.
      </p>
      <p class="hero-body">
        <strong>Current terminology note:</strong> The original teaching content is preserved, while current names are used where appropriate—for example, <em>Rhodococcus equi</em> for the organism historically called <em>Corynebacterium equi</em>, <em>Trueperella pyogenes</em> for older <em>Corynebacterium/Arcanobacterium pyogenes</em> terminology, and <em>Mycobacterium avium</em> subsp. <em>paratuberculosis</em> (MAP) for Johne’s disease.
      </p>

      <article id="staph" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Staphylococcal Infections</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Pyogenic lesions, dermatitis, and suppurative exudation</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>S. pseudintermedius</em> (canine/feline), <em>S. aureus</em> (bovine/equine), <em>S. hyicus</em> (porcine).</li>
              <li>Commensal skin/mucosa; invades via epithelial barrier disruption.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Coagulase production, hemolysins, and exoproteins induce neutrophil influx and necrosis.</li>
              <li>Exfoliative toxins cause epidermal detachment (<em>S. hyicus</em>).</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Abscesses, pyoderma, greasy exudative dermatitis, suppurative mastitis.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Suppurative inflammation, bacterial clusters (cocci in tetrads/clusters).</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 2: STREPTOCOCCUS -->
      <article id="strep" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Streptococcal Infections</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Suppurative lymphadenitis, polyserositis, and septicaemia</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>S. equi</em> subsp. <em>equi</em> (Strangles), <em>S. suis</em> (porcine polyserositis/meningitis), <em>S. agalactiae</em>.</li>
              <li>Upper respiratory tract, lymph nodes, and mucosal surfaces.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Antiphagocytic M-proteins, capsule formation, and streptolysins facilitate regional dissemination.</li>
              <li>Vascular damage leads to purulent exudation and bacteremia.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Mandibular/retropharyngeal abscesses, fibrinopurulent polyserositis, purpura haemorrhagica.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Dense neutrophilic infiltrates, fibrin deposition, chains of cocci.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 3: CORYNEBACTERIUM & TRUEPERELLA -->
      <article id="coryne" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Corynebacterium &amp; Trueperella</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Caseous lymphadenitis and chronic pyogenic abscessation</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis</em> (small ruminants/equine), <em>Trueperella pyogenes</em> (formerly <em>Corynebacterium/Arcanobacterium pyogenes</em>; cattle and other domestic species).</li>
              <li>Lymph nodes, visceral organs, and damaged tissue sites.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Lipid-rich cell wall confers resistance to phagocytic killing.</li>
              <li>Phospholipase D exotoxin enhances vascular permeability and lymphatic spread.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Concentric "onion-skin" caseous lymph nodes, chronic purulent lesions.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Pyogranulomatous inflammation with central caseous/suppurative necrosis.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 4: CLOSTRIDIUM -->
      <article id="clostridium" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Clostridial Diseases</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Histotoxic, enterotoxaemic, and neurotropic Anaerobic Spore-formers</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Clostridium chauvoei</em> (blackleg), <em>C. septicum</em> (malignant edema/braxy), <em>C. haemolyticum</em> (bacillary hemoglobinuria; historically <em>C. novyi</em> type D), <em>C. novyi</em> type B (black disease), <em>C. perfringens</em>, <em>C. tetani</em>, and <em>C. botulinum</em>.</li>
              <li>Skeletal muscle, intestinal mucosa, and neural tissue.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Potent exotoxins (alpha, beta, epsilon) cause cell membrane lysis, necrosis, or neuromuscular blockage.</li>
              <li>Anaerobic tissue injury promotes spore germination.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Darkened emphysematous muscle, necrotising enteritis, dark blood-tinged fluid.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Myonecrosis, gas vacuolation, mucosal necrosis, large Gram-positive rods.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

          <figcaption>Blackleg in cattle caused by Clostridium chauvoei. Affected muscles show necrosis, gas formation, and dark discoloration. The bacteria are Gram-positive, spore-forming rods.</figcaption>
        </figure>

      <!-- UNIT 5: BACILLUS -->
      <article id="bacillus" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Bacillus anthracis (Anthrax)</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Peracute septicaemia, vascular collapse, and splenomegaly</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Bacillus anthracis</em> (spore-forming aerobic rod).</li>
              <li>Systemic vascular endothelium and reticuloendothelial system.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Poly-D-glutamic acid capsule prevents phagocytosis.</li>
              <li>Tripartite toxin (Protective Antigen, Lethal Factor, Edema Factor) causes vascular damage and shock.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Dark non-clotting blood from orifices, severe splenomegaly ("blackberry jam" spleen).</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Massive bacteremia in capillaries, tissue edema, widespread hemorrhage.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      
        <figure class="illustration">
          <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1P4pgBbbXBXuIOtZVqMZF23SvnQi-zZ_5" alt="Anthrax in cattle caused by Bacillus anthracis. Key features include rapid death, splenomegaly, and dark, unclotted blood. The organism is a large Gram-positive rod with a capsule." loading="lazy">
          <figcaption>Anthrax in cattle caused by Bacillus anthracis. Key features include rapid death, splenomegaly, and dark, unclotted blood. The organism is a large Gram-positive rod with a capsule.</figcaption>

          <div class="disease-subtitle">Chronic staphylococcal pyogranulomatous infection</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Distribution</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>The chapter describes botryomycosis primarily in horses but also in dogs, cattle, sheep and cats, associated mainly with <em>Staphylococcus</em> infection.</li>
              <li>Infection commonly follows wounds and usually affects subcutaneous tissues, although internal organs can be involved.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Firm fibroma-like swellings may contain pockets and fistulous tracts filled with yellow pus.</li>
              <li>Small pale bacterial “grains” can be present within the exudate.</li>
              <li>Recognized sites include the spermatic cord after castration (scirrhous cord), tail, udder and sternal/shoulder regions.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Bacterial colonies occupy the center of pyogranulomas and may be surrounded by eosinophilic Splendore-Hoeppli material.</li>
              <li>Neutrophils surround the colonies, followed by macrophages and lymphocytes, with peripheral granulation tissue and fibrosis.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>

        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Hosts</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Rhodococcus equi</em> is a Gram-positive facultative intracellular pathogen and an important cause of pneumonia in young foals.</li>

          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">

            <ul>
              <li>Virulent organisms survive and replicate in macrophages, producing chronic suppurative to pyogranulomatous bronchopneumonia.</li>

            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">

            <ul>
              <li>Multiple firm pulmonary nodules/abscesses may be scattered throughout affected lungs or coalesce into larger lesions.</li>
              <li>Histologically, alveoli contain numerous macrophages with neutrophils and giant cells, accompanied by necrosis and pyogranulomatous inflammation.</li>
              <li>Current definitive diagnosis commonly combines lower-airway sampling with culture and PCR detection of virulence-associated markers such as <em>vapA</em>.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>

            <ul>
              <li>In horses, <em>C. pseudotuberculosis</em> can cause chronic lower-limb lymphangitis with nodules/abscesses aligned along lymphatic vessels.</li>
              <li>Nodules may rupture and form persistent ulcers; lymphatics become thickened and corded.</li>

            <ul>
              <li><strong><em>Corynebacterium renale</em> group:</strong> associated with ascending suppurative cystitis, ureteritis and pyelonephritis, especially in cattle.</li>
              <li><strong><em>Trueperella pyogenes</em>:</strong> causes suppurative infections and abscesses in many tissues, including lung, liver, kidney, joints and reproductive tract.</li>

      <article id="actinomycosis" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Actinomycosis (Lumpy Jaw)</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle"><em>Actinomyces bovis</em> — chronic suppurative and proliferative osteomyelitis</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Entry</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Actinomyces bovis</em> is a branching, slender Gram-positive bacterium and a commensal of the oral and gastrointestinal tract.</li>
              <li>Mucosal wounds caused by coarse feed or foreign material allow organisms to invade deeper tissues and bone.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Hard, irregular, immovable enlargement of mandibular or maxillary bone produces the classic “lumpy jaw.”</li>
              <li>Osteolysis and reactive bone formation create a honeycombed appearance; sinus tracts may drain yellow pus containing sulfur granules.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Central bacterial colonies are surrounded by radiating eosinophilic Splendore-Hoeppli material.</li>
              <li>Neutrophils and epithelioid macrophages are followed by giant cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells and dense peripheral fibrosis.</li>
              <li>The combination of pyogranulomatous inflammation and osteomyelitis distinguishes it from soft-tissue actinobacillosis.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      </article>

      <!-- MYCOBACTERIAL DISEASES -->
      <article id="mycobacteria" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Mycobacterial Diseases — Acid-Fast Gram-Positive Lineage</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Tuberculosis and paratuberculosis (Johne’s disease)</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Tuberculosis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Veterinary tuberculosis is caused primarily by members of the <em>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</em> complex, especially <em>M. bovis</em>, with <em>M. avium</em> complex important in birds and several mammalian hosts.</li>
              <li>These organisms are acid-fast and do not stain reliably with routine Gram methods because of their lipid-rich cell wall.</li>
              <li>Inhalation is a major route; ingestion can also occur. Macrophage infection leads to cell-mediated immune responses and granuloma formation.</li>
              <li>Classic tubercles contain central caseous necrosis that may mineralize, surrounded by epithelioid macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, lymphocytes and fibrosis.</li>
              <li>Primary lesions plus involvement of draining regional lymph nodes form the primary complex; lymphatic/hematogenous spread may produce miliary or generalized TB.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Johne’s Disease</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Caused by <em>Mycobacterium avium</em> subsp. <em>paratuberculosis</em> (MAP).</li>
              <li>Chronic granulomatous enteritis mainly affects ruminants; infection is often acquired when animals are young, with a long preclinical period.</li>
              <li>Gross lesions range from subtle change to diffusely thickened, corrugated distal small intestine with enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes and serosal lymphangitis.</li>
              <li>Histologically, mucosa/submucosa are expanded by epithelioid macrophages and giant cells containing variable numbers of acid-fast bacilli.</li>
              <li>Current diagnosis relies heavily on organism detection, particularly PCR, supplemented by culture, ELISA and histopathology according to disease stage and herd context.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>High-Yield Difference</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Tuberculosis:</strong> discrete granulomas/tubercles with caseation and often mineralization; lesions commonly involve respiratory tract and regional lymph nodes in cattle.</li>
              <li><strong>Johne’s disease:</strong> diffuse granulomatous enteritis, usually without the classic caseating tubercles seen in bovine TB.</li>
              <li>Both require acid-fast methods and organism-specific testing rather than routine Gram stain alone.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      </article>

        <strong>Gram-Positive Diagnostic Key:</strong> Begin with lesion pattern and bacterial morphology: clusters of cocci suggest staphylococci, chains of cocci suggest streptococci, large spore-forming rods support clostridial or bacillary disease, and branching rods suggest actinomycetes. 
        Pyogranulomatous lesions require special attention to Splendore-Hoeppli material and tissue distribution. Mycobacteria are acid-fast organisms with lipid-rich walls and should be investigated with acid-fast staining plus organism-specific culture or molecular testing rather than relying on routine Gram stain.
