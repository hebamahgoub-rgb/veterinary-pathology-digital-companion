      <h1>Gram-Negative Bacterial Diseases</h1>
      <div class="hero-subtitle">Enteric, respiratory, vascular, and zoonotic pathogens</div>
      <p class="hero-body">
        Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer lipopolysaccharide (LPS) membrane containing lipid A endotoxin. In veterinary pathology, endotoxaemia triggers severe systemic inflammation, endotoxic shock, microthrombosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), fibrinous polyserositis, and fibrinecrotic enteritis across animal species.
      </p>
      <p class="hero-body">
        <strong>Current terminology note:</strong> This page preserves the teaching content while using current names where appropriate—for example, <em>Glaesserella parasuis</em> (formerly <em>Haemophilus parasuis</em>) and <em>Burkholderia mallei</em> for glanders. Recent diagnostic and pathogenesis updates are integrated without removing the original disease profiles.
      </p>

      <!-- UNIT 1: ENTEROBACTERIACEAE -->
      <article id="enterobacteriaceae" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Enterobacteriaceae (<em>Escherichia coli</em> &amp; <em>Salmonella</em>)</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Colibacillosis, salmonellosis, enterotoxaemia, and septicaemia</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>ETEC, EPEC, STEC/EHEC (Colibacillosis in neonates); <em>Salmonella enterica</em> serovars (Typhimurium, Dublin, Choleraesuis).</li>
              <li>Intestinal mucosa, vascular endothelium, liver, and systemic circulation.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Enterotoxins induce hypersecretion or mucosal necrosis; endotoxin release causes sepsis and shock.</li>
              <li>Invasive <em>Salmonella</em> survives within macrophages, leading to systemic dissemination.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Fibrinous enteritis, "button ulcers" (porcine salmonellosis), paratyphoid nodules, septicaemic petechiae.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Mucosal necrosis, fibrinous thrombosis, paratyphoid granulomas in liver, bacterial rods in vessels.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 2: PASTEURELLACEAE -->
      <article id="pasteurellaceae" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Pasteurellaceae (<em>Mannheimia</em> &amp; <em>Pasteurella</em>)</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Fibrinous pleuropneumonia, enzootic pneumonia, and haemorrhagic septicaemia</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Mannheimia haemolytica</em>, <em>Pasteurella multocida</em>, <em>Bibersteinia trehalosi</em>.</li>
              <li>Respiratory mucosa, cranioventral lung lobes, pleura, and vasculature.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Leukotoxin (LKT) selectively destroys bovine leukocytes and platelets; LPS damages endothelium.</li>
              <li>Synergistic action with primary viral (e.g., IBR, BRSV) or stress factors ("Shipping Fever").</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Marbled, red-to-grey pulmonary consolidation, fibrinous pleuritis, pulmonary necrosis.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Fibrinous alveolitis, oat-shaped "oat cells" (elongated degraded leukocytes), interlobular edema.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 3: HISTOPHILUS & HAEMOPHILUS -->
      <article id="histophilus-haemophilus" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Histophilus &amp; Haemophilus Infections</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Thrombotic meningoencephalitis (TME), Glässer disease, and polyserositis</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Histophilus somni</em> (bovine histophilosis: TME, pneumonia, myocarditis and other systemic forms), <em>Glaesserella parasuis</em> (formerly <em>Haemophilus parasuis</em>; swine Glässer disease).</li>
              <li>Endothelium, central nervous system, synovial membranes, and serosal cavities.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>H. somni</em> adheres to vascular endothelium, promoting endothelial injury, platelet adhesion and predominantly in-situ thrombosis; ischemia and infarction may follow in brain, myocardium, pleura, synovium and other tissues.</li>
              <li><em>G. parasuis</em> is commonly carried in the upper respiratory tract of pigs; under stress or coinfection, virulent strains may invade systemically and cause fibrinous polyserositis, polyarthritis and meningitis.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Multifocal brain infarctions/haemorrhages (TME), fibrinous polyserositis, arthritis.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Suppurative vasculitis, fibrinoid necrosis of blood vessels, intravascular microthrombi.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 4: PSEUDOMONAS & BURKHOLDERIA -->
      <article id="pseudomonas-burkholderia" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Pseudomonas &amp; Burkholderia</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Opportunistic necrotising infections, glanders, and melioidosis</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Pseudomonas aeruginosa</em> (mastitis/otitis/wounds), <em>Burkholderia mallei</em> (Glanders in equids), <em>B. pseudomallei</em>.</li>
              <li>Respiratory tract, skin, lymphatics, and compromised epithelial surfaces.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Exotoxin A inhibits protein synthesis; proteases and elastases destroy host tissue matrix.</li>
              <li><em>B. mallei</em> causes pyogranulomatous lymphangitis and mucosal ulceration.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Blue-green suppurative exudate, "farcy buds" (nodular cutaneous lymphangitis), nasal septal ulcers.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Necrotising vasculitis, pyogranulomatous nodules with central nuclear debris (karyorrhexis).</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      </article>

      <!-- UNIT 5: BRUCELLA & FRANCISELLA -->
      <article id="brucella-francisella" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Brucella &amp; Francisella</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Granulomatous placentitis, reproductive failure, and zoonotic tularaemia</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Brucella abortus</em>, <em>B. melitensis</em>, <em>B. suis</em>, <em>B. canis</em>; <em>Francisella tularensis</em>.</li>
              <li>Reproductive tract (trophoblasts, epididymis), reticuloendothelial organs (spleen, liver, lymph nodes).</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Facultative intracellular bacterial tropism for erythritol in placental tissue.</li>
              <li>Replication inside macrophages induces persistent granulomatous or necrotising inflammation.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> Leathery necrotic cotyledons, intercotyledonary edema, epididymitis, focal hepatic necrosis.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Necrotising and granulomatous placentitis, introcytoplasmic bacteria in trophoblasts, miliary necrotic foci.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      
        <div class="illustration-group">
        <figure class="illustration-card">

      <!-- UNIT 6: CAMPYLOBACTER & LAWSONIA -->
      <article id="campylobacter-lawsonia" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Campylobacter &amp; Lawsonia</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Vibrionic abortion, intestinal adenomatosis, and proliferative enteropathy</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Campylobacter fetus</em> (subsp. <em>fetus</em> / <em>venerealis</em>), <em>Lawsonia intracellularis</em> (swine/equine).</li>
              <li>Placenta, ileal enterocytes, and mucosal crypt epithelium.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>C. fetus</em>: Endovascular infection of placenta causing necrotic placentitis and fetal hypoxia.</li>
              <li><em>L. intracellularis</em>: Intracellular infection of crypt cells stimulating enterocyte proliferation and mucosal thickening.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Gross:</strong> "Targetoid" necrotic lesions in fetal liver (Campylobacter); thickened "cerebriform" intestinal mucosa (Lawsonia).</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> Placentitis and vasculitis; crypt epithelial hyperplasia with loss of goblet cells and curved intracellular rods.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 7: ACTINOBACILLUS -->
      <article id="actinobacillus" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Actinobacillus Infections</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Actinobacillosis (woody tongue) and <em>Actinobacillus equuli</em> infection</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Actinobacillus lignieresii</em> is a small Gram-negative bacillus associated particularly with soft-tissue infection in cattle.</li>
              <li>Penetrating wounds or plant material allow entry, with frequent involvement of tongue musculature and regional lymph nodes.</li>
              <li><em>A. equuli</em> is an important cause of septicemic disease in foals and may infect joints, kidneys, lungs and other organs.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>A. lignieresii</em> induces pyogranulomatous inflammation and progressive fibrosis in affected soft tissues.</li>
              <li>The characteristic eosinophilic radiating clubs represent Splendore-Hoeppli material deposited around bacterial colonies.</li>
              <li>In young foals, <em>A. equuli</em> may enter around birth and produce septicemia with embolic localization in kidneys and joints.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Woody tongue:</strong> enlarged, firm tongue with multifocal granulomatous abscesses, small yellow-white foci and diffuse fibrosis.</li>
              <li><strong>Micro:</strong> bacterial colonies surrounded by radiating eosinophilic clubs, neutrophils, epithelioid macrophages and giant cells, with an outer fibrous response.</li>
              <li><strong><em>A. equuli</em>:</strong> septicemic lesions in young foals, purulent arthritis, and multiple small cortical renal abscesses caused by bacterial emboli.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      
        <div class="illustration-group">
        <figure class="illustration-card">
          <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1D4j6WewfsFm15mIlyE6pBnS__IBc69LB" alt="Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae in Pigs" loading="lazy">
          <figcaption class="illustration-caption"><strong>Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae in Pigs.</strong> Severe necrotizing pneumonia caused by Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae, a major respiratory pathogen in pigs.</figcaption>
        </figure>
        </div>

      <!-- UNIT 8: LEPTOSPIROSIS -->
      <article id="leptospirosis" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Leptospirosis</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle">Systemic, renal, hepatic and reproductive leptospiral disease</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Transmission</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Pathogenic <em>Leptospira</em> spp. are slender motile spirochetes capable of infecting most mammalian species.</li>
              <li>Transmission commonly follows exposure of mucous membranes or damaged skin to urine-contaminated water or environments; venereal and transplacental transmission can also occur.</li>
              <li>Maintenance hosts may carry organisms in renal tubules or the genital tract and shed leptospires for prolonged periods.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Leptospiremia is followed by replication in multiple tissues, particularly kidneys, liver, lungs, genital tract and sometimes the CNS.</li>
              <li>Acute disease may produce vascular injury, hemolysis, hepatic dysfunction and renal tubular damage.</li>
              <li>Persistent renal colonization is important in carrier states; reproductive localization contributes to abortion and other reproductive losses.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Dog:</strong> icterus and petechiae may occur; kidneys can show tubular epithelial degeneration and desquamation, progressing to tubulointerstitial nephritis.</li>
              <li><strong>Cattle:</strong> hemolytic anemia, hemoglobinuria, icterus and renal cortical lesions may occur; abortion is an important manifestation.</li>
              <li>Silver impregnation and modern molecular methods can demonstrate or detect leptospires, while diagnosis commonly combines PCR with serology.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      
        <div class="illustration-group">
        <figure class="illustration-card">
          <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1_5RAYnwJVrVprKB3GQC7Cn6r-8mAs8JK" alt="Leptospira Interstitial Nephritis" loading="lazy">
          <figcaption class="illustration-caption"><strong>Leptospira Interstitial Nephritis.</strong> Interstitial nephritis caused by Leptospira spp., a common gram-negative spirochete affecting multiple species.</figcaption>
        </figure>
        </div>

      </article>

      <!-- UNIT 9: NECROBACILLOSIS -->
      <article id="necrobacillosis" class="disease-card">
        <header class="disease-header">
          <h3 class="disease-title">Necrobacillosis</h3>
          <div class="disease-subtitle"><em>Fusobacterium necrophorum</em> — necrotizing mucosal, hepatic and cutaneous disease</div>
        </header>
        <div class="pathology-grid">
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Aetiology &amp; Tropism</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><em>Fusobacterium necrophorum</em> is a Gram-negative obligate anaerobe and a normal component of ruminant gastrointestinal flora.</li>
              <li>Damaged, necrotic and poorly oxygenated tissue favors invasion and multiplication.</li>
              <li>Important sites include oral and pharyngeal mucosa, rumen, liver, respiratory tract and damaged skin/feet.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Pathogenesis</h4>
            <ul>
              <li>Local tissue damage allows invasion, with bacterial virulence factors promoting necrosis and extension of infection.</li>
              <li>In feedlot cattle, rumenitis associated with high-concentrate feeding can permit portal spread of <em>F. necrophorum</em> to the liver.</li>
              <li>Hepatic lesions progress from focal necrosis to encapsulated abscesses; complications may include caudal vena caval thrombosis.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
          <div class="pathology-box">
            <h4>Gross &amp; Microscopic Lesions</h4>
            <ul>
              <li><strong>Calf diphtheria:</strong> well-demarcated gray-yellow necrotic plaques affecting oral, pharyngeal or laryngeal mucosa, often extending deeply.</li>
              <li><strong>Rumen-liver abscess complex:</strong> necrotizing rumenitis followed by focal hepatic necrosis and abscess formation.</li>
              <li><strong>Horse:</strong> necrotizing dermatitis may involve the heel and deeper structures of the foot under chronically wet, contaminated conditions.</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>
      </article>

    <!-- 4. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH PANEL -->
    <section class="section-block">
      <aside class="diagnostic-panel" aria-label="Diagnostic Key">
        <strong>Gram-Negative Diagnostic Key:</strong> Gram staining typically demonstrates pink/red rods or coccobacilli, but morphology and Gram stain alone are not sufficient for definitive diagnosis. 
        Correlate the dominant lesion pattern with tissue tropism: fibrinous polyserositis (<em>Glaesserella</em>), endothelial thrombosis and infarction (<em>Histophilus</em>), fibrinonecrotizing bronchopneumonia (<em>Mannheimia</em>), enteritis/septicemia (<em>E. coli</em> and <em>Salmonella</em>), reproductive and granulomatous lesions (<em>Brucella</em>), pyogranulomatous soft-tissue disease (<em>Actinobacillus</em>), tubulointerstitial renal injury (<em>Leptospira</em>), and necrotizing lesions/abscesses (<em>Fusobacterium</em>). 
        Silver stains can aid visualization of selected slender or intracellular organisms, while culture, PCR and serology are used according to the suspected agent.
