      <h1>Protein Accumulations and Hyaline Change</h1>
      <p>Morphological description of homogeneous, glassy, intensely eosinophilic proteinaceous deposits.</p>
    </header>

    <!-- A. ESSENTIAL DEFINITION -->
    <section class="section">
      <div class="essential-box">
        <h2>Essential Definition</h2>
        <div class="essential-alert">
          “Hyaline” describes a homogeneous, glassy, smooth, bright pink (eosinophilic) appearance on standard H&amp;E stain. It is a purely descriptive physical appearance, NOT a single chemically defined compound or specific disease diagnosis.
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- B, C, D. CLASSIFICATION & EXAMPLES -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Classification &amp; Veterinary Examples</h2>
      <div class="class-grid">
        
        <div class="class-card">
          <h3>1. Intracellular Hyaline</h3>
          <p style="font-size: 0.875rem; color: var(--secondary-color); margin-bottom: 8px;">Accumulations located within the cytoplasm of parenchymal cells:</p>
          <ul>
            <li><strong>Renal Protein Reabsorption Droplets:</strong> Eosinophilic droplets in proximal tubular epithelium following glomerular protein leakage (proteinuria).</li>
            <li><strong>Russell Bodies:</strong> Globular, condensed immunoglobulin deposits expanding the endoplasmic reticulum of plasma cells (Mott cells).</li>
            <li><strong>Viral Inclusion Bodies:</strong> Intranuclear or intracytoplasmic proteinaceous aggregates induced by viral replication (e.g., Canine Distemper).</li>
            <li><strong>Zenker's Degeneration:</strong> Eosinophilic, glassy, homogeneous sarcoplasm in damaged skeletal muscle fibers (e.g., Vitamin E/Selenium deficiency).</li>
          </ul>
        </div>

        <div class="class-card">
          <h3>2. Extracellular Hyaline</h3>
          <p style="font-size: 0.875rem; color: var(--secondary-color); margin-bottom: 8px;">Deposits located in connective tissue, vessel walls, or extracellular matrix:</p>
          <ul>
            <li><strong>Vascular Wall Hyalinization:</strong> Plasma protein insudation into arteriolar walls (arteriolosclerosis) secondary to chronic hypertension or endothelial injury.</li>
            <li><strong>Connective Tissue Hyalinization:</strong> Dense, homogeneous, acellular collagenous matrix forming in chronic scars, fibrous adhesions, or old corpora albicantia.</li>
            <li><strong>Glomerular Hyalinization:</strong> Accumulation of plasma proteins and matrix material in chronically damaged renal glomeruli (end-stage glomerulosclerosis).</li>
          </ul>
        </div>

      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- E. PATHOGENESIS -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Pathogenetic Mechanisms</h2>
      <div class="mech-grid">
        <div class="mech-card">
          <strong>Excessive Reabsorption</strong>
          Excessive endocytosis of filtered plasma proteins by renal proximal tubular cells exceeding lysosomal clearance capacity.
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <strong>Plasma Leakage (Insudation)</strong>
          Endothelial barrier breakdown allowing plasma proteins (fibrinogen, immunoglobulins, complement) to leak into vascular walls or interstitial space.
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <strong>Excess Protein Synthesis</strong>
          Overproduction and condensation of secretory proteins (e.g., immunoglobulins) within dilated rough endoplasmic reticulum cisternae.
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <strong>Collagen Alteration</strong>
          Cross-linking, compaction, and loss of fibrillar detail in aging or chronically injured dense fibrous connective tissue.
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- F. MICROSCOPIC MORPHOLOGY -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Microscopic Features (H&amp;E)</h2>
      <div class="morph-box">
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Homogeneous Texture:</strong> Smooth, uniform, structureless appearance with complete loss of normal cellular or collagenous fibrillar details.</li>
          <li><strong>Glassy Refractility:</strong> Refractile, clear, pink appearance under standard light microscopy.</li>
          <li><strong>Intense Eosinophilia:</strong> Stains bright pink-red with hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E) due to high protein concentration.</li>
          <li><strong>Morphological Forms:</strong> Occurs as distinct intracellular rounded droplets, extracellular vascular rings, or broad connective tissue sheets.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- G. DIAGNOSTIC CAUTION -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Diagnostic Caution</h2>
      <div class="caution-box">
        <strong>Important Note for Pathologists:</strong> Because "hyaline" is purely an optical descriptor, encountering hyaline material on H&amp;E requires evaluating anatomical location, clinical history, and often special histochemical or immunohistochemical stains (e.g., Masson's trichrome, PTAH, Congo red, or specific antibody stains) to determine the exact biochemical identity (e.g., collagen, fibrin, amyloid, or immunoglobulins).
      </div>
    </section>

        <h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>"Hyaline" is a descriptive physical term for smooth, homogeneous, bright pink, glassy material on H&amp;E stain.</li>
          <li>May be intracellular (e.g., protein reabsorption droplets, Russell bodies) or extracellular (e.g., vascular hyalinization, chronic scar tissue).</li>
          <li>Requires location, clinical history, and special stains to differentiate from amyloid, fibrin, collagen, or mineral deposits.</li>
        </ul>

        <h3>Knowledge Check</h3>
        
        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>1. Is hyaline a single chemical substance or a specific etiology?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            No. "Hyaline" is purely a descriptive morphological term denoting any smooth, homogeneous, glassy, eosinophilic appearance on H&amp;E.
          </details>
        </div>

        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>2. What is a Russell body, and in which cell type is it identified?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            A Russell body is a large, rounded, eosinophilic intracellular hyaline inclusion composed of condensed immunoglobulins within the rough endoplasmic reticulum of a plasma cell (forming a Mott cell).
          </details>
        </div>

        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>3. What causes renal protein reabsorption droplets to form in proximal tubular epithelial cells?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            Glomerular disease causing severe proteinuria forces proximal tubular cells to pinocytose excess filtered plasma proteins, forming bright pink cytoplasmic droplets.
          </details>
