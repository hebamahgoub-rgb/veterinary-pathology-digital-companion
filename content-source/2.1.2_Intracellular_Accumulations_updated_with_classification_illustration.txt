      <p>Overview of cellular mechanisms leading to the storage of endogenous and exogenous substances within parenchymal cells.</p>

    <!-- A. INTRODUCTION -->
    <section class="section">
      <div class="intro-card">
        <h2>Pathological Principles</h2>
        <p>
          Intracellular accumulation develops when cells are unable to adequately metabolize, package, transport, secrete, or degrade organic or inorganic material. The stored substances may reside within the cytoplasm, organelles (such as lysosomes), or the nucleus, resulting in structural alterations and functional compromise.
        </p>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- B. GENERAL MECHANISMS -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">General Pathogenetic Mechanisms</h2>
      <div class="mech-grid">
        <div class="mech-card">
          <h3>1. Abnormal Metabolism</h3>
          <p>A normal endogenous substance is produced at a normal or increased rate, but metabolic pathways or transport systems are inadequate to clear it (e.g., hepatic lipidosis).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <h3>2. Defective Protein Folding / Transport</h3>
          <p>An abnormal endogenous protein accumulates due to gene mutations causing protein misfolding, impaired endoplasmic reticulum transport, or lack of secretion (e.g., alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <h3>3. Enzyme Deficiency</h3>
          <p>A normal or abnormal endogenous substance accumulates because a inherited or acquired enzymatic defect prevents its normal degradation (e.g., lysosomal storage diseases).</p>
        </div>
        <div class="mech-card">
          <h3>4. Indigestible Material Ingestion</h3>
          <p>An exogenous substance accumulates because the cell lacks the enzymatic machinery to degrade it or the ability to transport it out of the cell (e.g., carbon particles / silica).</p>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- C. TYPES OF ACCUMULATED MATERIAL -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Categories of Accumulated Material</h2>
      <div class="types-grid">
        <div class="type-card">
          <strong>Normal Endogenous Produced in Excess</strong>
          Water, lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates generated faster than their clearance rate.
        </div>
        <div class="type-card">
          <strong>Normal Endogenous Unmetabolized</strong>
          Substances accumulating due to specific metabolic blockades or enzyme failure.
        </div>
        <div class="type-card">
          <strong>Abnormal Endogenous Substances</strong>
          Misfolded proteins, mutant enzymes, or abnormal metabolic byproducts.
        </div>
        <div class="type-card">
          <strong>Exogenous Materials</strong>
          Inhaled carbon, mineral dusts, heavy metals, or indigestible bacterial pigments.
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- F. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH -->
    <section class="section">
      <h2 class="section-title">Diagnostic Approach Sequence</h2>
      <div class="approach-box">
        <p style="font-size: 0.95rem; color: var(--secondary-color);">
          Follow this systematic sequence when evaluating microscopic tissue sections displaying cytoplasmic vacuolation or accumulation:
        </p>
        <div class="approach-steps">
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 1: Localization</strong> &mdash; Determine whether the material is strictly intracellular, extracellular, or both.
          </div>
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 2: Color and Texture</strong> &mdash; Assess if the material is clear, eosinophilic, basophilic, or naturally pigmented.
          </div>
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 3: Vacuole Borders</strong> &mdash; Evaluate vacuolar margins (sharply demarcated vs. feathery/indistinct).
          </div>
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 4: Nuclear Position</strong> &mdash; Check whether the nucleus remains centrally located or is peripherally displaced.
          </div>
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 5: Special Stains</strong> &mdash; Select the appropriate histochemical stain (e.g., Oil Red O, PAS with diastase, Congo Red).
          </div>
          <div class="approach-step">
            <strong>Step 6: Clinical Correlation</strong> &mdash; Interpret the morphological findings in light of species, clinical history, and laboratory data.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div class="quiz-box">
        <h3>Knowledge Check</h3>
        
        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>1. What key morphological feature on H&E helps distinguish macrovesicular fatty change from glycogen accumulation?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            Macrovesicular fatty change forms sharply demarcated, clear, round vacuoles that displace the nucleus to the cell periphery, whereas glycogen accumulation produces feathery, indistinct clear spaces with a centrally retained nucleus.
          </details>
        </div>

        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>2. Why are special lipid stains (such as Oil Red O) performed on frozen tissue sections rather than routine paraffin sections?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            Routine paraffin processing uses organic solvents (alcohol and xylene) that dissolve and wash away neutral lipids, leaving empty vacuoles. Frozen sectioning avoids these solvents.
          </details>
        </div>

        <div class="quiz-q">
          <p>3. Which mechanism explains carbon particle (anthracosis) accumulation in pulmonary alveolar macrophages?</p>
          <details>
            <summary>Show Answer</summary>
            Ingestion of an indigestible exogenous material that the cell lacks enzymatic pathways to degrade or transport out.
          </details>
        </div>
