    <p>Abnormal deposition of insoluble calcium salts within soft tissues.</p>
  </header>

  <figure class="teaching-figure">

  </figure>

  <section class="section">
    <div class="definition-box">
      <h2>Definition</h2>
      <p>Pathological calcification (mineralization) is the abnormal deposition of insoluble calcium salts (primarily calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate) in tissues other than osteoid or enamel. It is divided into two distinct pathophysiological types: <strong>dystrophic calcification</strong> and <strong>metastatic calcification</strong>.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Types of Calcification</h2>
    <div class="calc-grid">
      <div class="calc-column">
        <div class="calc-card">
          <h3>Dystrophic Calcification</h3>
          <p>Occurs locally in <strong>dead, dying, or degenerate tissues</strong> despite normal systemic calcium metabolism and normal serum calcium levels.</p>
          <ul>
            <li>Ischemic or coagulative necrotic tissue (e.g., myocardial or renal infarcts).</li>
            <li>Enzymatic fat necrosis (e.g., acute pancreatitis).</li>
            <li>Caseous cores of chronic granulomatous lesions (e.g., tuberculosis).</li>
            <li>Dead or encysted parasites and chronic vascular thrombi.</li>
          </ul>
        </div>
        <figure class="teaching-figure">
          <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1vfTJB8ux2IxjqC1LkjlorYm_aiJ39aRu" alt="Mechanism of dystrophic calcification in necrotic tissue">
          <figcaption><strong>Mechanism of Dystrophic Calcification.</strong> A necrotic cell is depicted with membrane disruption permitting calcium influx. Swollen mitochondria accumulate calcium, initiating crystal formation within injured tissue. This figure illustrates the hallmark process of dystrophic calcification, which occurs despite normal systemic calcium levels.</figcaption>
        </figure>
      </div>

      <div class="calc-column">
        <div class="calc-card">
          <h3>Metastatic Calcification</h3>
          <p>Occurs in <strong>previously normal living tissues</strong> secondary to systemic mineral imbalances characterized by <strong>hypercalcemia</strong> or an elevated calcium-phosphorus product.</p>
          <ul>
            <li>Affects widespread vascular and elastic tissues.</li>
            <li>Target organs typically secrete or concentrate acids, creating a local alkaline environment favoring calcium precipitation.</li>
            <li>Common target sites: Gastric mucosa, renal tubular basement membranes, pulmonary alveolar walls, systemic arteries, and endocardium.</li>
          </ul>
        </div>
        <figure class="teaching-figure">
          <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1o_uyFOjx6XpNfbC_ANDFtabiIDzCBnBS" alt="Mechanism and sites of metastatic calcification">
          <figcaption><strong>Mechanism of Metastatic Calcification.</strong> Normal tissues are shown developing fine calcium deposits along basement membranes under conditions of elevated blood calcium. The illustration emphasizes systemic mineral imbalance as the driver of metastatic calcification, independent of local tissue injury.</figcaption>
        </figure>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Veterinary Causes of Metastatic Calcification</h2>
    <div class="causes-grid">
      <div class="cause-card"><strong>Vitamin D Toxicity</strong>Ingestion of cholecalciferol rodenticides or calcinogenic plants (e.g., <em>Solanum malacoxylon</em>, <em>Cestrum diurnum</em>) driving excessive intestinal calcium absorption.</div>
      <div class="cause-card"><strong>Chronic Renal Failure</strong>Renal phosphate retention leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and widespread soft tissue calcification (rubber jaw, gastric mineralization).</div>
      <div class="cause-card"><strong>Primary Hyperparathyroidism</strong>Functional parathyroid adenomas overproducing PTH, driving osteoclast resorption and severe hypercalcemia.</div>
      <div class="cause-card"><strong>Paraneoplastic Hypercalcemia</strong>Secretion of PTH-related protein (PTHrp) by tumors such as anal sac adenocarcinoma (AGASACA) or lymphoma.</div>
      <div class="cause-card"><strong>Osteolytic Bone Disease</strong>Multiple myeloma or widespread metastatic bone tumors releasing skeletal calcium stores into circulation.</div>
    </div>
    <figure class="teaching-figure">
      <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1_NNHupaoG-_CqUDJZc1Lj_PsJsH76U12" alt="Major causes of hypercalcemia leading to metastatic calcification">
      <figcaption><strong>Major Causes of Hypercalcemia.</strong> A four-icon summary of systemic conditions that elevate blood calcium and predispose to metastatic calcification: renal failure, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D toxicity, and neoplastic bone destruction.</figcaption>
    </figure>
  </section>

  <section class="section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Dystrophic versus Metastatic Calcification</h2>
    <div class="table-responsive">
      <table class="data-table">
        <thead><tr><th>Feature</th><th>Dystrophic Calcification</th><th>Metastatic Calcification</th></tr></thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td><strong>Condition of Tissue</strong></td><td>Necrotic, dying, or degenerate tissue</td><td>Previously normal, viable tissue</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Serum Calcium Levels</strong></td><td>Normal</td><td>Elevated (Hypercalcemia)</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Systemic Mineral Balance</strong></td><td>Normal</td><td>Abnormal (Hypercalcemia / Hyperphosphatemia)</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Distribution</strong></td><td>Localized to foci of injury/necrosis</td><td>Widespread / Systemic</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Principal Mechanism</strong></td><td>Membrane breakdown, phosphate accumulation, calcium binding</td><td>Precipitation of mineral due to systemic saturation</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Common Sites</strong></td><td>Infarcts, fat necrosis, granulomas, dead parasites</td><td>Gastric mucosa, lungs, kidneys, arteries, endocardium</td></tr>
          <tr><td><strong>Veterinary Examples</strong></td><td>Pancreatic saponification, white muscle disease</td><td>Vitamin D toxicity, renal hyperparathyroidism, AGASACA</td></tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>

  <section class="section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Morphological Features</h2>
    <div class="morph-grid">
      <div class="morph-card"><h3>Gross Morphology</h3><ul><li><strong>Appearance:</strong> Irregular white, pale, or chalky deposits.</li><li><strong>Texture:</strong> Gritty, firm, or hard plaques and granules.</li><li><strong>Handling:</strong> Produces a distinctive gritting sensation when sectioned with a scalpel blade.</li><li><strong>Vessels / Organs:</strong> Affected arterial walls or endocardium become rigid, thickened, and lose elasticity.</li></ul></div>
      <div class="morph-card"><h3>Microscopic Morphology (H&amp;E)</h3><ul><li><strong>Intracellular / Extracellular:</strong> Amorphous, granular, or solid deposits.</li><li><strong>Color on H&amp;E:</strong> Deeply basophilic (dark purple to blue-black).</li><li><strong>Tissue Reaction:</strong> May elicit a chronic foreign-body giant-cell reaction or peripheral fibrous encapsulation over time.</li><li><strong>Location:</strong> Concentrated within necrotic debris (dystrophic) or along basement membranes/elastic fibers (metastatic).</li></ul></div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Histochemical Confirmation</h2>
    <div class="stain-box">
      <p style="font-size:.95rem;color:var(--secondary-color);">While prominent basophilic mineral deposits are readily visible on standard H&amp;E stains, specific histochemical stains confirm the presence of mineral salts:</p>
      <div class="stain-grid">
        <div class="stain-card"><strong>Von Kossa Stain</strong>Stains carbonate and phosphate anions associated with calcium deposits intense dark brown to black. (Standard surrogate stain for mineral).</div>
        <div class="stain-card"><strong>Alizarin Red S</strong>Reacts directly with calcium cations to form a vivid red-orange chelate complex.</div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="section">
    <div class="takeaways-box">
      <h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
      <ul><li>Pathological calcification appears gross as chalky gritty white foci and microscopically as deep basophilic (purple) deposits on H&amp;E.</li><li>Dystrophic calcification occurs locally in dead/necrotic tissue with normal serum calcium.</li><li>Metastatic calcification occurs widespread in normal viable tissues secondary to hypercalcemia or altered calcium-phosphorus products.</li></ul>
    </div>
    <div class="quiz-box">
      <h3>Knowledge Check</h3>
      <div class="quiz-q"><p>1. What key clinical laboratory finding differentiates metastatic calcification from dystrophic calcification?</p><details><summary>Show Answer</summary>Systemic hypercalcemia (or altered calcium-phosphorus product). Serum calcium is elevated in metastatic calcification, whereas it remains normal in dystrophic calcification.</details></div>
      <div class="quiz-q"><p>2. Why are the gastric mucosa, lungs, and kidneys frequent target organs for metastatic calcification?</p><details><summary>Show Answer</summary>These tissues lose acid (H<sup>+</sup>) or concentrate solutes, creating a local alkaline internal environment that lowers mineral solubility and favors calcium salt precipitation.</details></div>
      <div class="quiz-q"><p>3. What color does calcium mineral deposit appear on standard H&amp;E stained tissue sections?</p><details><summary>Show Answer</summary>Deeply basophilic (dark purple to blue-black), amorphous or granular deposits.</details></div>
