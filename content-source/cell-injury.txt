    <!-- 1. START HERE -->
    <div id="start-here" class="start-here-box">
      <h2>1. Start Here</h2>
      <p>Normal cells maintain homeostatic balance. Physiological stress or pathological stimuli induce <strong>cellular adaptation</strong>. If adaptive limits are exceeded or if the stimulus is acutely toxic, <strong>cell injury</strong> ensues. Injury progresses from a <strong>reversible phase</strong> to an <strong>irreversible threshold</strong>, culminating in cell death via <strong>necrosis</strong> or <strong>apoptosis</strong>.</p>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1ycI0r-7F8LVAAzjqfpSyvCr1wFEPWZb6" alt="Overview of reversible and irreversible cell injury">
        <figcaption><strong>Reversible vs Irreversible Cell Injury.</strong> Overview of cellular responses to injury. Normal cells under mild stress develop reversible injury characterized by hydropic change, organelle swelling, and membrane blebbing. Persistent or severe injury leads to irreversible damage marked by loss of membrane integrity, mitochondrial amorphous densities, and nuclear breakdown (pyknosis &rarr; karyorrhexis &rarr; karyolysis), culminating in cell death.</figcaption>
      </figure>

    <!-- 2. CAUSES OF CELL INJURY -->
    <section id="causes" class="topic-section">
      <h2>2. Causes of Cell Injury</h2>
      <p>Cellular injury stems from a spectrum of etiological factors operating individually or synergistically:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Oxygen Deprivation (Hypoxia &amp; Ischemia):</strong> Impairs oxidative phosphorylation; the most common cause of cell injury.</li>
        <li><strong>Physical Agents:</strong> Mechanical trauma, temperature extremes, radiation, and electrical shock.</li>
        <li><strong>Chemical Agents &amp; Toxins:</strong> Environmental pollutants, heavy metals, plant toxins, poisons, and therapeutic drugs.</li>
        <li><strong>Infectious Agents:</strong> Viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and helminths.</li>
        <li><strong>Immunologic Reactions:</strong> Autoimmune responses, hypersensitivity reactions, and immune-mediated tissue injury.</li>
        <li><strong>Genetic Derangements:</strong> Inborn errors of metabolism, chromosomal abnormalities, and congenital malformations.</li>
        <li><strong>Nutritional Imbalances:</strong> Deficiencies (e.g., Vitamin E/Selenium) or excesses (e.g., obesity, hepatic lipidosis).</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 3. MECHANISMS OF CELL INJURY -->
    <section id="mechanisms" class="topic-section">
      <h2>3. Mechanisms of Cell Injury</h2>
      <p>Biochemical events target critical intracellular systems:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>ATP Depletion:</strong> Failure of energy-dependent membrane pumps (Na+/K+ ATPase), leading to intracellular sodium accumulation and cell swelling.</li>
        <li><strong>Mitochondrial Damage:</strong> Loss of membrane potential and formation of high-conductance transition pores, releasing cytochrome c into the cytosol.</li>
        <li><strong>Calcium Influx:</strong> Loss of calcium homeostasis activates damaging intracellular enzymes (phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases, ATPases).</li>
        <li><strong>Oxidative Stress:</strong> Accumulation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) resulting in lipid peroxidation of membranes, protein misfolding, and DNA damage.</li>
        <li><strong>Membrane Permeability Defects:</strong> Direct or enzymatic disruption of plasma, mitochondrial, and lysosomal membranes.</li>
      </ul>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/1LNBjH8cYh3TFCd9bRyD3d-lQRZGfhzd0" alt="Major biochemical mechanisms of cell injury">
        <figcaption><strong>Mechanisms of Cell Injury.</strong> Major biochemical mechanisms of cell injury. ATP depletion impairs ion pumps, causing cellular swelling. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce lipid peroxidation and protein/DNA damage. Loss of calcium homeostasis activates destructive enzymes. Mitochondrial injury triggers permeability transition and loss of oxidative phosphorylation, driving the cell toward irreversible injury and death.</figcaption>
      </figure>

    </section>

    <!-- 4. REVERSIBLE CELL INJURY -->
    <section id="reversible" class="topic-section">
      <h2>4. Reversible Cell Injury</h2>
      <p>Morphological alterations that can return to baseline if the damaging stimulus is removed.</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Cellular Swelling (Hydropic Change):</strong> First manifestation; paleness, increased organ weight, microvacuolated cytoplasm under light microscopy.</li>
        <li><strong>Fatty Change (Lipidosis):</strong> Appearance of lipid vacuoles in hepatocytes and cardiomyocytes following metabolic disruption.</li>
        <li><strong>Ultrastructural Changes:</strong> Plasma membrane blebbing, microvilli blunting, mitochondrial swelling, and ribosomal detachment.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 5. IRREVERSIBLE CELL INJURY -->
    <section id="irreversible" class="topic-section">
      <h2>5. Irreversible Cell Injury</h2>
      <p>The point of no return is characterized biochemicaly and morphologically by two main features:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Inability to reverse mitochondrial dysfunction:</strong> Persistent ATP depletion.</li>
        <li><strong>Profound membrane disturbance:</strong> Severe damage to plasma, lysosomal, and organelle membranes with massive calcium influx and loss of cellular proteins.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 6. NECROSIS -->
    <section id="necrosis" class="topic-section">
      <h2>6. Necrosis</h2>
      <p>Uncontrolled cell death resulting from severe acute injury, accompanied by enzyme digestion and inflammatory response in surrounding viable tissue.</p>
      <h3>Nuclear Alterations in Necrosis</h3>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Pyknosis:</strong> Nuclear shrinkage and increased basophilia (dense, dark nucleus).</li>
        <li><strong>Karyorrhexis:</strong> Fragmentation of the pyknotic nucleus.</li>
        <li><strong>Karyolysis:</strong> Dissolution of the nucleus due to DNase activity (fading basophilia).</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 7. PATTERNS OF NECROSIS -->
    <section id="patterns" class="topic-section">
      <h2>7. Patterns of Necrosis</h2>
      <p>The gross and microscopic appearance reflects the tissue architecture and underlying etiology:</p>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/15oy5ar7iDQuORHzTsLupC-F7_7k-bLHW" alt="Morphologic patterns of necrosis">
        <figcaption><strong>Morphologic Patterns of Necrosis.</strong> Morphologic patterns of necrosis. Coagulative necrosis preserves tissue architecture despite protein denaturation. Liquefactive necrosis results in enzymatic digestion and fluidification, especially in the CNS. Caseous necrosis combines features of coagulative and liquefactive necrosis, producing friable, granular material. Fat necrosis involves lipase-mediated destruction of adipocytes and formation of chalky calcium soaps.</figcaption>
      </figure>

      
      <div class="pattern-grid">
        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Coagulative Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Denaturation of structural proteins predominates over enzymatic digestion. Tissue architecture is preserved for days ("ghost outlines"). Typical of ischemic injury (infarction) in solid organs except the brain.</p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Liquefactive Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Enzymatic digestion predominates over protein denaturation. Rapidly transforms tissue into a liquid viscous mass. Typical of CNS ischemic necrosis (malacia) and bacterial/fungal abscesses.</p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Caseous Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Friable, "cheese-like" gross appearance. Complete loss of tissue architecture replaced by granular acellular debris. Classic for granulomatous inflammation (e.g., <em>Mycobacterium</em>, Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis).</p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Fat Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Focal areas of fat destruction resulting from pancreatic lipase release (pancreatitis) or trauma. Fatty acids combine with calcium (saponification) to form chalky-white deposits.</p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Fibrinoid Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Microscopic pattern seen in immunopathic vascular reactions. Antigen-antibody complexes deposit in arterial walls alongside fibrin, producing a bright pink, homogeneous microscopic appearance.</p>
        </div>

        <div class="pattern-card">
          <h3>Gangrenous Necrosis</h3>
          <p>Clinical term applied to a limb or organ that has lost blood supply and undergone necrosis. Classified into <strong>Dry Gangrene</strong> (ischemic coagulative) and <strong>Wet Gangrene</strong> (bacterial liquefactive superinfection).</p>
        </div>
      </div>
    </section>

    <!-- 8. APOPTOSIS -->
    <section id="apoptosis" class="topic-section">
      <h2>8. Apoptosis</h2>
      <p>Regulated individual cell death mechanism requiring energy (ATP) activation without eliciting an inflammatory response. Operates via two main pathways:</p>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Intrinsic (Mitochondrial) Pathway:</strong> Initiated by cell stress, DNA damage, or growth factor withdrawal. Cytochrome c leaks into cytosol, forming the apoptosome and activating <em>Caspase-9</em>.</li>
        <li><strong>Extrinsic (Death Receptor) Pathway:</strong> Triggered by cell surface receptors (Fas, TNFR1) binding ligands (FasL, TNF-&alpha;), activating <em>Caspase-8</em>.</li>
        <li><strong>Execution Phase:</strong> Executioner caspases (Caspase-3, -6, -7) cleave cellular targets, leading to cell fragmentation into apoptotic bodies cleared by phagocytes.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <!-- 9. COMPARISON TABLE -->

    <!-- 10. CELLULAR ADAPTATIONS TO STRESS -->
    <section id="adaptations" class="topic-section">
      <h2>10. Cellular Adaptations to Stress</h2>
      <ul>
        <li><strong>Hypertrophy:</strong> Increase in cell size leading to increased organ size (e.g., cardiac hypertrophy).</li>
        <li><strong>Hyperplasia:</strong> Increase in cell number in response to stimulus (e.g., hormonal epidermal hyperplasia).</li>
        <li><strong>Atrophy:</strong> Decrease in cell size and organ volume due to disuse, denervation, diminished blood supply, or inadequate nutrition.</li>
        <li><strong>Metaplasia:</strong> Reversible substitution of one adult cell type for another (e.g., squamous metaplasia of respiratory epithelium in Vitamin A deficiency).</li>
      </ul>

      <figure class="teaching-figure">
        <img src="https://lh3.googleusercontent.com/d/12GAPflFrJW9yqNiWbRBpoqpn8283Opn7" alt="Subcellular responses to injury including autophagy cytoskeletal damage and lipid accumulation">
        <figcaption><strong>Subcellular Responses to Injury.</strong> Subcellular responses to injury. Autophagy removes damaged organelles through lysosomal degradation. Cytoskeletal injury disrupts cellular structure and transport. Lipid accumulation (steatosis) occurs when injured cells cannot adequately metabolize or export lipids, producing visible vacuoles within the cytoplasm.</figcaption>
      </figure>

    </section>

    <section id="gallery" class="topic-section">
      <h2>11. Practical Pathology Gallery</h2>
      <p>Curated gross and microscopic cases demonstrating patterns of cell injury and death.</p>

        <strong>Comparative pathology:</strong> Selected human specimens are included where they clearly demonstrate fundamental pathological processes shared across species. Each human-derived example is explicitly labelled.
      </div>

              <strong>Teaching Point:</strong> Unlike infarcts in most solid organs, cerebral infarcts undergo enzymatic liquefaction and may ultimately leave a cystic cavity.

            <ul class="case-meta">
              <li><strong>Organ:</strong> Lymph Node (Small Ruminant)</li>
              <li><strong>Pattern:</strong> Caseous Necrosis</li>
              <li><strong>Etiology:</strong> <em>Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis</em></li>
            </ul>
            <p>A chronic abscess in a ewe contains thick, dry-to-pasty caseous material. Older lesions may develop laminated or onion-ring organisation.</p>
            <div class="teaching-point">
              <strong>Teaching Point:</strong> Caseous necrosis combines elements of coagulative and liquefactive necrosis, producing friable, cheese-like debris.
            </div>
          </div>
        </div>

            <div class="case-title">Pancreatic Fat Necrosis</div>
            <ul class="case-meta">
              <li><strong>Organ:</strong> Peripancreatic Adipose Tissue</li>
              <li><strong>Pattern:</strong> Enzymatic Fat Necrosis</li>
              <li><strong>Stain:</strong> H&amp;E</li>
            </ul>
            <p>Necrotic adipose tissue is transformed into eosinophilic, poorly defined material accompanied by acute inflammatory cells. Calcium soaps may form within affected fat.</p>
            <div class="teaching-point">
              <strong>Teaching Point:</strong> Activated pancreatic lipases hydrolyze triglycerides into free fatty acids that bind calcium (saponification).
            </div>
          </div>
        </div>

            <div class="case-title">Dry Gangrene Following Frostbite</div>
            <ul class="case-meta">
              <li><strong>Site:</strong> Distal Extremities</li>
              <li><strong>Pattern:</strong> Ischemic Gangrenous Necrosis</li>
              <li><strong>Etiology:</strong> Severe cold injury</li>

            <p>Both distal extremities show dark, dry, sharply demarcated devitalised tissue following severe cold-induced vascular injury.</p>
            <div class="teaching-point">
              <strong>Teaching Point:</strong> Dry gangrene represents predominantly coagulative ischemic necrosis of an extremity before secondary bacterial infection and liquefaction develop.
            </div>
          </div>
        </div>

              <li><strong>Pattern:</strong> Apoptotic keratinocytes and epidermal necrosis</li>

            <p>Small epidermal foci contain apoptotic keratinocytes and nuclear debris, demonstrating the condensed and fragmented nuclear material associated with programmed cell death.</p>

              <strong>Teaching Point:</strong> Apoptotic cells appear individually as shrunken cells or condensed nuclear fragments. In mixed lesions, apoptosis may occur beside areas of tissue necrosis.
