Between Endemic Vaccination and Export Walls: What Egypt and France Reveal About Living with H5N1

Egypt's poultry industry feeds a domestic market of more than 110 million people under endemic avian influenza and imported feed pressure. France spends €100 million a year vaccinating ducks to keep outbreaks at bay—and still pays a trade price abroad.

Few animal diseases expose the fault line between food security and international trade as sharply as highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1. Between July 2025 and March 2026 alone, 185 A(H5N1) animal events were reported to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH), spanning 1,204 poultry outbreaks, 6,326 wild-bird detections, and nine bovine outbreaks worldwide, driven predominantly by clades 2.3.4.4 and 2.3.2.1. For decades, exporting nations treated poultry vaccination as a last resort that endangered trade status, while endemic countries relied on it to keep affordable protein on the table. In 2026, those two worlds are converging—and comparing Egypt with France shows exactly what vaccination can, and cannot, buy.

Egypt: High Self-Sufficiency Built on Endemic Management and Imported Feed

Poultry is the backbone of animal protein in Egypt. According to industry analysis grounded in USDA Foreign Agricultural Service (FAS) Cairo GAIN reporting, Egypt produced 1.6 billion commercial broilers and 320 million rural birds in 2025—covering 97% of domestic broiler demand—alongside 16 billion table eggs, or roughly 140 eggs per person. That represents a substantial rebound above the pre-2023 baseline of 1.4 billion birds and 14 billion eggs.

Yet that domestic self-sufficiency rests on two structural vulnerabilities. The first is economic: USDA's Cairo office describes the poultry sector as the primary driver of Egypt's corn and feed demand, meaning every expansion in flock size enlarges a compound-feed bill priced largely in foreign currency. The second is epidemiological: Egypt has been endemic for HPAI in poultry since 2006 and has relied on large-scale vaccination for nearly two decades.

Long-term analyses of Egypt's vaccination campaigns have repeatedly flagged a classic endemic dilemma: when vaccination becomes the primary control tool without equally intensive biosecurity, movement control, and active surveillance, passive outbreak reporting declines while antigenically drifting strains continue to circulate in backyard and mixed-species settings (Peyre et al.). A November 2026 serological survey of 444 livestock serum samples collected across three Egyptian governorates between October 2024 and January 2026 underscored that ecological reality: antibodies to HPAI H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b were detected in 2.9% to 4.5% of sampled animals—exclusively cattle—while H9N2 antibodies appeared across cattle (1.6%), goats (3.2%), and sheep (10.3%). Though seropositivity indicates past exposure rather than active clinical disease, it reinforces why backyard-to-livestock interfaces remain a One Health blind spot.

France: Preventive Vaccination Works Epidemiologically—at €100 Million a Year

Across the Mediterranean, France offers the clearest real-world test of preventive HPAI vaccination inside an export-oriented European market. After repeated clade 2.3.4.4b waves decimated its waterfowl industry—cutting foie gras output from roughly 18,000–19,000 tonnes to 9,000–10,000 tonnes in 2022—France launched mandatory commercial duck vaccination in October 2023 and renewed it on October 1, 2024, October 1, 2025, and October 1, 2026, covering 55 to 60 million ducks per twelve-month campaign.

Epidemiologically, the shift from crisis culling to prevention paid off rapidly. Poultry outbreaks fell to just 10 during the 2023–2024 season, with modeling estimating a 96% to 99% reduction in epizootic size (314 to 756 outbreaks averted), while French foie gras production rebounded above 16,800 tonnes in 2025. Even so, vaccination is neither cheap nor set-and-forget. As industry leaders told the World Egg Organisation in July 2026, France spends roughly €100 million annually (~€1.56 per bird), split 60% industry and 40% state—with roughly half of total expenditure going to surveillance rather than syringes. Under Technical Instruction 2026-585 for the 2026/2027 season, France also mandates a third booster dose in designated Special Risk Zones (Zones à Risque Particulier) to counter waning duck immunity around six weeks.

The Trade Friction That Remains

Although most importers accept heat-treated cooked duck products from vaccinated French flocks and the United Kingdom has reopened to raw duck meat, raw product remains restricted in the United States, Canada, Japan, Thailand, and South Korea (The Poultry Site, July 2026). Breeder flocks exporting hatching eggs or day-old ducklings are therefore kept outside the mandatory vaccination mandate. Even so, Europe's direction of travel is clear: in May 2026, Italy launched its first pilot HPAI H5 vaccination project in turkeys and laying hens in Veneto and Lombardy.

Pathology & Diagnostic Note: Why Surveillance Costs as Much as the Vaccine

In unvaccinated gallinaceous birds, clade 2.3.4.4b HPAI produces acute systemic endothelial injury, edema, petechial hemorrhages, and multifocal necrosis across visceral organs—making outbreaks unmistakable through sudden mortality spikes. Vaccination changes the clinical picture without always creating sterilizing immunity:

  • Silent shedding risk: Authorized clade 2.3.4.4b duck vaccines (such as Volvac B.E.S.T. AI+ND and Ceva Respons AI H5) markedly reduce clinical signs and viral excretion, yet wild-type incursions can still occur quietly (UK Joint Vaccination Taskforce, 2025).
  • DIVA compatibility and virological swabbing: To prove vaccinated flocks are free of field virus, France pairs DIVA-compatible platforms with monthly clinical examinations and systematic virological testing of vaccinated birds, plus tracheal or oropharyngeal swabbing of dead or moribund ducks in restricted zones (PoultryMed, Oct 2026).
  • Lesson for Egypt: For endemic producers, France's experience confirms that vaccines protect margins only when backed by active laboratory surveillance and compartment-level biosecurity (WOAH Egypt Compartmentalization)—otherwise hidden circulation accelerates antigenic drift and raises the long-term cost of every subsequent dose.
