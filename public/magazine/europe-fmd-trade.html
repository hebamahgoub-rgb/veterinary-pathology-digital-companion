The Price of Losing Disease-Free Status: Lessons from Europe's 2025–2026 Foot-and-Mouth Outbreaks

Between January 2025 and mid-2026, two separate waves of foot-and-mouth disease struck five European Union member states—triggering immediate export suspensions, mass culling, and over a million emergency vaccine doses. The crisis shows why international trade rules dictate a policy playbook opposite to endemic control.

Why a single farm can halt a nation's exports

Under World Trade Organization sanitary rules, the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) formally recognizes countries as "FMD-free where vaccination is not practised." That designation is the commercial key to exporting fresh meat, dairy, and live animals to high-value markets. The moment an outbreak is confirmed, official disease-free status is suspended—and veterinary authorities can no longer sign standard export health certificates for susceptible animal products.

Germany demonstrated how unforgiving that mechanism is in January 2025. On 10 January 2025, authorities confirmed FMD serotype O in a small herd of 14 water buffalo (3 dead) in Märkisch-Oderland, Brandenburg—Germany's first case since 1988 and the EU's first since Bulgaria in 2011 (GOV.UK Preliminary Outbreak Assessment; German Federal Ministry of Agriculture). Even though strict zoning prevented any second farm from becoming infected, third-country exports—including pig meat and dairy shipments to Great Britain—froze overnight until bilateral exemptions and two comprehensive WOAH dossiers restored Germany's FMD-free status on 14 April 2025.

Two waves across Europe: serotype O in 2025, SAT1 in 2026

What began as a contained German incident quickly widened into two distinct European waves, documented by the European Commission and mapped in Frontiers in Veterinary Science (2026):

Why trade recovery lags months behind viral containment

Even after the last infected herd is depopulated and surveillance tests return negative, trade does not snap back automatically. Inside the European Union's single market, regionalization allowed meat and dairy from outside restricted zones to keep moving (BMLEH). Outside the EU, however, importing countries apply independent biosecurity audits and exclusion windows.

Australia, for example, enforced exclusion periods on FMD-susceptible goods from Hungary (3 February to 10 September 2025) and Slovakia (3 February to 31 October 2025) (DAFF Australia). Similarly, while WOAH restored Slovakia's FMD-free status at the end of October 2025, the UK Office for SPS Trade Assurance did not formally re-recognize Slovakia's FMD freedom until 23 February 2026—nearly eleven months after the first Slovak case.

Egypt vs. Europe · One Virus, Two Policy Worlds

Europe avoids preventive FMD vaccination during peacetime because vaccinating herds forfeits "free without vaccination" export certificates—meaning authorities rely on rapid culling, movement freezes, and emergency vaccine banks only when outbreaks breach the border. Egypt, by contrast, is a net red-meat importer managing endemic circulation of four serotypes, where continuous vaccination protects domestic supply and smallholder livelihoods. Yet Wave 2 in Cyprus and Lesvos shows that both sides of the Mediterranean now face the same SAT1 threat—prompting joint Mediterranean preparedness talks co-chaired by Egypt alongside FAO and WOAH in June 2026.

What comes next: inside the tissue and the lab

Whether a country fights FMD through emergency stamping-out in Europe or multi-serotype vaccination in Egypt, every policy choice hinges on the same biological realities: how quickly the virus destroys oral and hoof epithelium, why calves can die suddenly from myocarditis, and why seven immunologically distinct serotypes defeat mismatched vaccines. Part 3 of this collection takes readers inside those pathological and diagnostic mechanisms.
